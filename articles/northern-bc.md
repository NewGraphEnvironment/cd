# How the Weather Has Changed in Three Northern BC Watersheds

Three watersheds in northern British Columbia, each feeding a different
great river: two run to the Pacific and one to the Arctic. This page
compares the weather of the last ten years, and of the last three, with
what was normal in 1951–1980. The short answer: **all three are clearly
warmer, they hold less snow, the snow melts sooner, and summers are
thirstier.** Total rain and snow changed by 5 % or less over ten years,
and the last three years swung between dry and wet.

Ten years is long enough to start to show the climate. Three years is
mostly weather, so the figures show each of the last three years on its
own, not just their average.

The upper Bulkley gets 758 mm of rain and snow in a normal year, against
1055 mm below Houston, and its typical peak snowpack is only 55 % as
deep. Over the last ten years it warmed by 1.9 °C and the lower Bulkley
by 2.0 °C, and both lost about the same share of their peak snowpack (9
% and 8 %). Summer rain over the last three years fell further upstream
(25 % less than normal, against 14 %). About the same warming and loss
of snow, landing on a drier upper watershed.

## What this means for watershed health

The changes above are the kind that studies elsewhere in western North
America have tied to how streams run and how warm they get. The table
names each source and the region it studied.

| What changed here | What it does to streams | Why fish care |
|----|----|----|
| Less snow, melting 9 to 13 days sooner | Snow-fed rivers across western North America now run one to four weeks earlier than in the 1950s to 1970s ([Stewart et al. 2005](#ref-stewart_etal2005ChangesEarlier)). In the Fraser, a shrinking snowpack moved the spring high water about ten days earlier and lowered summer flows ([Kang et al. 2016](#ref-kang_etal2016ImpactsRapidly)). | Lower summer flows in the Fraser come when salmon migrate upriver ([Kang et al. 2016](#ref-kang_etal2016ImpactsRapidly)). |
| Warmer summers, drier air and soil | Warmer summers raise stream temperatures, and summer flows run lower and warmer (projections for Washington State) ([Mantua et al. 2010](#ref-mantua_etal2010Climatechange)). | Warm water can block salmon and steelhead migration and raise their risk of disease ([Mantua et al. 2010](#ref-mantua_etal2010Climatechange)). Across the United States, warming is projected to shrink the stream habitat cool enough for trout and other cold-water fish by about half ([Eaton and Scheller 1996](#ref-eaton_scheller1996Effectsclimate)). |

Summer air dryness measures how strongly the air draws moisture from the
land. Higher values dry soils and make plants close their pores to save
water, which slows their growth; across the United States it rose from
1979 to 2013 ([Ficklin and Novick
2017](#ref-ficklin_novick2017Historicprojected)). The loss of spring
snowpack is not only local weather either: in four large BC river
basins, the Peace among them, the drop between 1961 and 2005 has been
attributed to human-caused climate change ([Najafi et al.
2017](#ref-najafi_etal2017AttributionObserved)).

## Next steps: from weather to water

Climate is the first half of the story. What streams actually do, how
much water they carry and how warm it gets, is the second half. That
work lives in the [wet](https://www.newgraphenvironment.com/wet/)
package, which turns records like these into streamflow and water
temperature.

## How this was made

The numbers come from ERA5-Land, a global reconstruction of past weather
on a grid of about 6.5 by 11 km at this latitude, averaged over each
watershed by the cd package. “Normal” is 1951–1980, the reference period
NASA uses for global temperature, and close to the start of the record.
Some warming had already happened by then, so these numbers understate
the change since pre-industrial times. The last 10 years are 2016–2025
and the last 3 are 2023–2025. The two regional reports, [FWCP Peace
Region](https://newgraphenvironment.github.io/cd/articles/peace-fwcp.md)
and [Kootenay
Lake](https://newgraphenvironment.github.io/cd/articles/kootenay-lake.md),
explain the method and its statistics in full; they compare 2015–2025,
so their numbers differ slightly from these. To reproduce the Bulkley’s
numbers:

``` r

library(cd)
aoi <- sf::st_read(system.file("extdata", "example_aoi_northern_bc.gpkg",
                               package = "cd"))
ts <- cd_extract(cd_catalog(), aoi[aoi$code == "BULK", ])
cd_compare(ts[ts$variable == "tmean", ], window_a = 2016:2025,
           window_b = 1951:1980, test = NULL)
cd_compare(ts[ts$variable == "prcp", ], window_a = 2016:2025,
           window_b = 1951:1980, method = "pct_change", test = NULL)
```
