## A. stewart_etal2005ChangesEarlier

**Claim:** Across western North America, the spring snowmelt pulse in snow-fed rivers has been arriving
earlier, by roughly one to four weeks over the second half of the 20th century, as winters and springs
warmed.

**Verdict: SUPPORTED** (one point of precision below)

Quotes:

- Abstract, p. 1136: "Widespread and regionally coherent trends toward earlier onsets of springtime
  snowmelt and streamflow have taken place across most of western North America, affecting an area that
  is much larger than previously recognized. These timing changes have resulted in increasing fractions
  of annual flow occurring earlier in the water year by 1–4 weeks. The immediate (or proximal) forcings
  for the spatially coherent parts of the year-to-year fluctuations and longer-term trends of streamflow
  timing have been higher winter and spring temperatures."
- Sect. 5 Conclusions, p. 1152: "These shifts represent an advance to earlier streamflow timing by one to
  four weeks in recent decades relative to conditions that prevailed in the 1950s through the mid-1970s.
  Earlier flows are observed throughout the mountainous parts of the region, but trends are strongest for
  midelevation gauges in the interior Northwest, western Canada, and coastal Alaska."
- Sect. 5, p. 1152: "the primary cause for the regionally coherent trends toward earlier snowmelt and
  streamflow timing is a broad-scale increase of winter and spring temperatures by about 1°–3°C over the
  past 50 years."

Study region: western North America. The domain was "watersheds in the western 11 mountainous states of
the conterminous United States, the five western Canadian provinces, and Alaska" (Sect. 3, p. 1138).
Records run 1948–2002, from 294 snowmelt-dominated gauges, 53 of them Canadian. BC is inside the
domain, but the paper reports no BC-specific result.

Precision notes:

- The one-to-four-week figure describes streamflow timing: the centre of mass of annual flow, and the
  share of flow arriving earlier. It does not describe the snowmelt-pulse onset date itself. The paper
  says "spring pulse onset dates could not be calculated for Canadian gauges" (Fig. 2 caption, p. 1141).
- It applies at most gauges, not all. The PDO explains part of the shift, and the paper ties the rest to
  warming (Sect. 5, p. 1152).

Optional tighter wording: "Across western North America, including western Canada, snow-fed rivers now
deliver their spring and early-summer flow one to four weeks earlier than in the 1950s to 1970s, mainly
because winters and springs have warmed."

## B. kang_etal2016ImpactsRapidly

**Claim:** In BC's Fraser River basin, a declining snowpack has been shifting streamflow earlier in the
year: a larger spring flow, and lower flow later in summer.

**Verdict: SUPPORTED**

Quotes:

- Abstract, p. 1: "Rapidly declining mountain snowpacks and earlier melt onsets result in a 10-day
  advance of the Fraser River's spring freshet with subsequent reductions in summer flows when up-river
  salmon migrations occur."
- Results, p. 2: "The observed (simulated) daily streamflow for the Fraser River at Hope during April and
  May exhibits increasing trends … in response to an earlier onset of snowmelt (Fig. 2a). In contrast,
  the results also show observed (simulated) decreasing trends in streamflow … over a prolonged period
  (June, July and August)."
- Results, p. 3: "The combined effects of the warmer air temperatures, reduced snowfall and hence peak SWE
  induce earlier melt of the snow and a rapid advance of the spring freshet detected in observations and
  the VIC model simulations."
- Discussion, p. 4: "changes in its type and timing coupled with warmer air temperatures have led to a
  significant decline in peak seasonal snowpack accumulation and an advance in the onset of melt during
  spring."

Study region: the Fraser River Basin in BC, gauged at Hope, with six sub-basins (Upper Fraser,
Thompson-Nicola, Quesnel, Stuart, Nautley, Chilko). Water years 1949–2006. The study combines gauge
observations with the VIC hydrologic model. This is southern and central BC, not the north. Total annual
flow did not change significantly (p. 2: "insignificant changes in total annual discharge values").

Minor note: the paper also gives higher spring and summer evapotranspiration as a cause of the lower
warm-season runoff (p. 4). The claim does not say snowpack is the only cause, so it stands.

## C. najafi_etal2017AttributionObserved

**Claim:** The decline in spring snowpack observed across British Columbia since the mid-20th century
has been attributed, at least in part, to human-caused warming.

**Verdict: PARTLY SUPPORTED.** The attribution is supported. The time span and the spatial scope are
not.

Quotes:

- Abstract, p. 4113: "A detection and attribution analysis on the multidecadal trend in snow water
  equivalent (SWE) has been conducted in four river basins located in British Columbia (BC). … Results of
  the detection and attribution analysis shows that these declines are attributable to the anthropogenic
  forcing, which is dominated by the effect of increases in greenhouse gas concentration, and that they
  are not caused by natural forcing due to volcanic activity and solar variability combined.
  Anthropogenic influence is detected in three of the four basins (Fraser, Columbia, and Campbell Rivers)
  based on the VIC-reconstructed SWE, and in all basins based on the manual snow survey records."
- Sect. 6 Conclusions, p. 4127: "The analyses show that the SWEApril_1st has declined over British
  Columbia consistent with simulations that include responses to the anthropogenic-forcing factors, and
  consistent with the expected influence of warming on the hydrological cycle. Warming is substantial in
  the basins that are studied and is also found to be attributable to anthropogenic forcing."
- Sect. 5, p. 4125, on the Peace: "The estimated scaling factor for the Peace River basin is also close
  to unity but has a somewhat wider uncertainty band that intersects zero, implying that it is not
  possible to formally detect the ALL-forcing signal in nSWE in that basin."
- Sect. 6, p. 4128: "Our analyses were conducted over the period of 1961–2005."

Study region: four BC river basins: the Fraser, the Peace, the Upper Columbia and the Campbell. The
period is 1961–2005. "Snowpack" here means 1 April snow water equivalent, normalised by October–March
precipitation (nSWE).

Problems with the claim as written:

1. "Since the mid-20th century": the analysis runs from 1961, not from about 1950.
2. "Across British Columbia": the paper covers four basins. It reports a result for "BC as a whole",
   but that whole is those four basins combined.
3. The Peace is the only northern BC basin in the study. There the signal was detected in the manual
   snow-survey records but not in the modelled reconstruction. A northern BC vignette should not imply a
   clean result for the north.
4. "At least in part" is more cautious than the paper, which says the declines "are attributable to the
   anthropogenic forcing." That is acceptable, but it understates the result.

Suggested wording: "In four large BC river basins, including the Peace, the drop in spring snowpack
between 1961 and 2005 has been attributed to human-caused climate change, mostly from rising greenhouse
gases. In the Peace basin the signal was clearer in snow-survey records than in modelled snowpack."

## D. mantua_etal2010Climatechange

**Claim:** Warmer summers raise stream temperatures, and lower, warmer late-summer flows put salmon and
other cold-water fish under thermal stress, through migration barriers and disease risk among other
effects.

**Verdict: PARTLY SUPPORTED.** The mechanism is supported. "Other cold-water fish" goes beyond the
source, and the source is a set of future projections for Washington State.

Quotes:

- Abstract, p. 187: "Simulations predict rising water temperatures will thermally stress salmon
  throughout Washington's watersheds, becoming increasingly severe later in the twenty-first century."
- Abstract, p. 188: "Diminishing streamflows and higher stream temperatures in summer will be stressful
  for stream-type salmon populations that have freshwater rearing periods in summer."
- Sect. 1, p. 189, on method (warmer air means warmer water): "we employ the statistical modeling
  approach of Mohseni et al. (1998) to relate past surface air temperatures to stream temperatures, and
  apply these relationships trained on past climate in conjunction with projections of future air
  temperatures to predict corresponding future stream temperatures."
- Sect. 2.4, p. 194: "Excessively warm waters can inhibit salmon migration and breeding patterns, and
  reduce cold-water refugia and connectivity. … Water temperatures exceeding 21–22°C can prevent
  migration. Furthermore, adult salmon become more susceptible to disease and the transmission of
  pathogens as temperatures rise, and prolonged exposure to stream temperatures across a threshold
  (typically near 21°C, but this varies by species) can be lethal for juveniles and adults"
- Sect. 7 Conclusions, p. 212: "a growing number of locations will experience increasingly longer periods
  with weekly average water temperatures that are high enough to cause thermal migration barriers and
  increase the risk of fish kills (> 21°C)."

Study region: Washington State, USA. The study uses modelled stream temperatures at 124 sites and
hydrologic projections under A1B and B1 emissions scenarios, against a 1970–1999 baseline. The fish
covered are Pacific salmon and steelhead. The paper has no BC data.

Problems with the claim as written:

- "Other cold-water fish": the paper covers salmon and steelhead only. It mentions other fish only as
  competitors and predators ("native and non-native warm water fish", p. 194).
- The paper projects future change. It says little about what has already happened. Present-tense
  wording is fine for the mechanism, but not as an observed trend.

Suggested wording: "Warmer summers raise stream temperatures. Lower, warmer summer flows put salmon and
steelhead under heat stress: warm water can block their migration and raise their risk of disease."

## E. eaton_scheller1996Effectsclimate

**Claim:** As streams warm, the habitat that stays cool enough for cold-water fish such as trout shrinks.

**Verdict: SUPPORTED**

Quotes:

- Abstract, p. 1109: "Results indicated that habitat for cold and cool water fish would be reduced by
  ~50%, and that this effect would be distributed throughout the existing range of these species."
  (The PDF's text layer renders "~" as a corrupted glyph. The body text on p. 1112 reads "nearly 50%".)
- Results, p. 1112: "A nearly 50% reduction in thermal habitat is projected for cold and cool water
  species, but only a 14.2% decrease is estimated for warm water fish."
- Fig. 2 caption, p. 1112: "USGS monitoring stations with suitable thermal habitat for rainbow trout
  before and after climate warming. … Before climate change, suitable habitat at 632 sampling sites …;
  after climate change, at 322 of 632 sites".
- Results, p. 1112: "Rainbow trout and white sucker distributions indicate that cold and cool water fish
  will be least affected in higher latitude or elevation locations. Suitable habitat will be eliminated
  by climate warming throughout the range of a species-north as well as south."

Study region: the contiguous ("lower 48") United States, using 1,700 USGS stream monitoring stations
and 57 fish species. The study is a modelled projection for a doubling of atmospheric CO2, using the
Canadian Climate Centre GCM. It is not an observed trend. Its cold-water guild includes brook, brown,
cutthroat and rainbow trout and several Pacific salmon (Table 1, p. 1111).

Note for the vignette: the paper finds cold-water fish "least affected in higher latitude or elevation
locations" (p. 1112). That is relevant context for northern BC, and the vignette should not imply the
US-wide figure applies there.

## F. ficklin_novick2017Historicprojected

**Claim:** Vapour pressure deficit, how strongly the air pulls water out of soil and plants, has been
rising, and higher values mean more drying stress on soils and vegetation.

**Verdict: PARTLY SUPPORTED.** The definition and the effect on vegetation are supported. The rising
trend is shown for the United States only. In a BC vignette an unqualified "has been rising" reads as a
statement about BC.

Quotes:

- Sect. 4 Summary and Conclusions, p. 2076: "VPD represents the ability of the air to extract moisture
  from the land surface".
- Sect. 1, p. 2061: VPD "is the measure of atmospheric desiccation strength … and is an important
  determinant of the atmospheric demand for water vapor."
- Sect. 1, p. 2061: "over nonvegetated or sparsely vegetated surfaces an increase in VPD will promote an
  increase in ET, which can affect soil moisture".
- Sect. 1, p. 2062: stomates "close to prevent excessive water loss in a desiccating atmosphere. …
  Declines in stomatal conductance with rising VPD represent a significant constraint on plant carbon
  uptake and productivity … VPD has also been identified as a critical determinant of tree mortality".
- Abstract, p. 2061: "Historically, VPD has increased for all seasons, driven by increases in es and
  declines in ea. The spring, summer, and fall seasons exhibited the largest areal extent of significant
  increases in VPD, which was largely concentrated in the western and southern portions of the U.S."
- Sect. 3.1, p. 2066: "overall, increases in VPD were observed for the majority of the study area … the
  only significant decreases in VPD were found in western Montana."

Study region: the contiguous United States. The historical record is 1979–2013, from gridded METDATA,
and the projections cover 2065–2099 under RCP 8.5. The paper has no Canadian data. The rise was not
uniform: it was concentrated in the US West and South, and some areas (western Montana, the upper Great
Plains in spring) went the other way.

Note on "pulls water out of soil and plants": the paper's own gloss is "the ability of the air to
extract moisture from the land surface". Plants respond by closing their stomata, which limits growth.
So the plant-side effect is water stress and reduced uptake, not a direct pull of water out of plants.

Suggested wording: "Vapour pressure deficit measures how strongly the air draws moisture from the land.
Across the United States it rose between 1979 and 2013, most of all in the West and South. Higher values
dry soils and make plants close their pores to save water, which slows their growth."
If the vignette then shows a VPD rise for northern BC, attribute that to the ERA5-Land data, not to this
paper.
